A design can look perfect on screen and still print badly. White slivers at the edge, text sliced by the cutter and blurry photos are all common and all avoidable. Three ideas cover most of the problem: bleed, safe margins and resolution.

## Bleed

Printing and cutting are not perfectly exact. When a sheet is trimmed to size, the blade can move slightly. If your background colour or image stops right at the edge of the page, a tiny white line may appear where the cut lands early.

Bleed solves this. It is extra background that extends beyond the final size, which is trimmed away. A common amount is around 3 mm on every side, but printers differ, so ask yours.

How to use it:

1. Set up the document with bleed included, or enlarge the artwork on every side.
2. Extend backgrounds and images all the way to the edge of the bleed.
3. Keep anything important well inside the page.

## Safe margins

The safe margin is the opposite idea. It is a border inside the final size where no text, logo or important detail should go. If trimming shifts a little, the content stays intact.

A margin of about 5 mm is a typical starting point, and more is fine for small items. Fold lines also need care: keep text away from creases, so it does not disappear into a fold.

## Resolution

Resolution is the amount of detail in an image. Screens can look fine at lower detail, but print needs more. A common target for print is 300 pixels per inch at the final size.

- **Do not stretch small images.** A small picture enlarged to poster size will look soft or blocky.
- **Check logos.** Use vector files where possible, because they stay sharp at any size.
- **Mind screenshots.** Images saved from social media are usually small and compressed, so they rarely print well.

## Colour and fonts

Prepare the file in a mode that suits print and check the colours against a proof, as explained in our post on [RGB and CMYK](rgb-vs-cmyk-screen-vs-print.html). Either embed your fonts or convert text to outlines when sending to a printer, so nothing is replaced by a default font.

## File format

PDF is the most common format for print because it keeps layout, fonts and images together. Ask your printer whether they prefer a particular PDF setting.

## A short pre-flight checklist

1. Is the size correct, with bleed added?
2. Is all important content inside the safe margin?
3. Are images high enough resolution?
4. Are fonts embedded or outlined?
5. Have you read every word again for typos?
6. Have you asked the printer if they have any special requirements?
