Giving feedback on design is harder than it looks. You can feel that something is off without knowing why, and the words you reach for, such as "make it pop" or "it needs more energy", can mean very different things to different people. Better feedback leads to better design, and a faster process.

## Start with the goal, not the opinion

Remember what the design is meant to do. Does it need to sell a product, announce an event or build trust? Judge the work against that goal. "I like it" and "I don't like it" say little. "This is hard to read from a distance, and people need to read it from the street" gives the designer something to solve.

## Describe the problem, not the solution

Designers are trained to solve problems. If you describe what you see and how it affects you, they can find the best fix. Compare these:

- "Make the logo bigger."
- "I can barely notice our name, and I want people to remember it."

The second may lead to a bigger logo, or to a better placement, or to a stronger contrast. It gives the designer room to find what works.

## Be specific

Vague feedback causes extra rounds. Point to exact places and say what you notice.

- Instead of "the colours feel wrong", try "the orange feels too bright next to the blue, and I want the design to feel calmer."
- Instead of "the text is odd", try "the second line is hard to read because it is small and close to the picture."

## Say what is working

Mention what you like, too. It tells the designer what to keep, so the next version does not lose it. It also makes the whole conversation more positive.

## Gather opinions before sending

If several people are involved, combine their comments first and resolve disagreements yourself. Conflicting instructions waste revision rounds. Our post on [design revisions](how-many-design-revisions-are-fair.html) explains why.

## Separate taste from fit

Personal taste matters, but the design is for your audience, not only for you. Ask who it is for and whether they would respond well. Showing it to a few people who match your customer can be more useful than your own first reaction.

## Use examples

If words fail, show pictures. A reference of something you like, with a sentence on what you like about it, is often clearer than a paragraph of description. Use references for inspiration, not for copying.

## Take a pause

If a design feels wrong at first sight, wait a day and look again. First reactions can be about surprise, not quality.

The best working relationships come from honest, kind communication. Clear feedback is a sign of respect for the work and for the person doing it. If you have a project in mind, [let's work together](../contact.html).
