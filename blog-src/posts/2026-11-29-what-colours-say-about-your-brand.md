People notice colour before they read a single word. That makes it one of the fastest ways a brand can communicate how it wants to feel. Choosing well is less about trends and more about fit.

## Colours carry associations, not rules

Colours tend to bring certain feelings to mind, but these are tendencies, not laws. They also change across cultures and industries. Treat the list below as a starting point for thinking, not a guarantee.

- **Red** often feels energetic, urgent or appetising. It is common in food and sales.
- **Orange** tends to feel warm, friendly and playful.
- **Yellow** can feel optimistic and attention-grabbing.
- **Green** is often linked to nature, growth, health and calm.
- **Blue** commonly suggests trust, stability and clarity.
- **Purple** is often used for creativity, luxury or imagination.
- **Black** can feel premium, serious or modern.
- **White and light neutrals** feel clean, simple and spacious.

## Start from your brand, not from the wheel

Before picking colours, describe your brand in three words. Friendly, bold and affordable will lead somewhere different from calm, refined and expert. Then ask who the audience is and what competitors already use. Standing out can be an advantage, but only if the colours still feel right.

## Build a palette with roles

A palette is more useful when each colour has a job.

1. **A main colour** that people will associate with you.
2. **A supporting colour** to add variety.
3. **Neutrals** for backgrounds and text, such as off-white and near-black.
4. **An accent** used sparingly to draw attention to buttons or key details.

A good rule of thumb is to let neutrals do most of the work, the main colour take a clear share, and the accent appear rarely so it keeps its power.

## Check readability

A beautiful palette is not useful if the text cannot be read. Test combinations of text and background for contrast, especially for small text on phones. Our post on [colour contrast](colour-contrast-for-designers.html) explains how.

## Test it in real places

Look at your colours on a logo, a flyer, a social post, a website and a printed item. Colours can look different on screen and on paper, and a shade that glows on your phone may print duller. Always test a small print before a big order.
