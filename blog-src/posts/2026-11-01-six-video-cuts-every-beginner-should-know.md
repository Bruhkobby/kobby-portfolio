Editing is mostly about deciding where one shot ends and the next begins. The way you make that transition, the *cut*, changes how your video feels: smooth and invisible, sharp and energetic, or full of meaning. Here are six cuts every beginner should know, and when to use each.

You can do all of them in almost any editing software, whether that is DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro or a phone app.

## 1. Cut on action

You cut in the middle of a movement, such as someone reaching for a door handle, and pick it up in the next shot as their hand grabs it. The viewer's eye follows the motion, so the edit hides itself.

**Use it when** you want a smooth, natural flow between two angles of the same action.

**Tip:** film the action from more than one angle, so you have something to cut to.

## 2. Jump cut

A jump cut is a sudden, obvious transition that breaks visual continuity. The classic example is cutting out pauses from someone speaking to camera, so the same framing "jumps" forward in time.

**Use it when** you want to tighten talking-head videos, show time passing, or create energy and tension on purpose.

**Tip:** make it clearly deliberate. A small, half-hearted jump feels like a mistake. Changing the framing slightly, with a zoom in or out, can make it look intentional.

## 3. J-cut

In a J-cut, the audio of the *next* clip starts before its picture appears. The viewer hears what is coming while still seeing the current shot. On the timeline, the audio extends to the left of the edit point, which gives the "J" shape.

**Use it when** you want to lead the viewer into a new scene, build anticipation, or make a transition feel smooth.

## 4. L-cut

An L-cut is the opposite. The audio from the *current* clip carries on after the picture has cut to the next shot. On the timeline, the audio extends to the right of the edit point, making an "L".

**Use it when** you want to show someone's reaction while another person is still talking, or to keep a voiceover running across a series of images.

**Tip:** J-cuts and L-cuts are used constantly in interviews and documentaries because they make dialogue feel natural instead of chopped up.

## 5. Match cut

A match cut moves from one shot to another while keeping something the same, such as a shape, a movement, or the composition. It links two ideas or moments in the viewer's mind. A well-known example is the cut in *2001: A Space Odyssey* from a thrown bone to a spacecraft.

**Use it when** you want to connect two things visually, suggest a relationship, or show time passing in a creative way.

**Tip:** it takes planning. Think about the match while you are filming, not only in the edit.

## 6. Cutaway

A cutaway leaves your main subject for a different shot, often called B-roll, then returns to the original scene. For example, while someone describes a product, you cut to a close-up of it, then back to them.

**Use it when** you want to add context, hide a rough edit, or keep the video visually interesting while someone talks.

**Tip:** always film extra B-roll. It is the editor's most useful safety net.

## Putting them together

Try this in your next edit:

1. Start by **cutting on action** for any movement.
2. Use **J-cuts and L-cuts** wherever people are talking.
3. Cover rough edits with a **cutaway**.
4. Use a **jump cut** to remove pauses, and make it deliberate.
5. Save the **match cut** for moments where you want a bit of style.

## Less is more

Do not use every technique in every video. The best editing is often the kind nobody notices. Choose a cut because it serves the story, not to show off.
