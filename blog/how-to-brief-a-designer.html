How to Brief a Designer: 10 Things to Include for Better Results

A clear brief saves time, money and frustration. Use this 10-point checklist to tell your designer exactly what you need, and get better work first time.

CategoryWorking Together
Published29 Oct 2026
Reading time4 min

Most design problems do not start in the design. They start in the brief. When a designer is not told who the work is for, what it should achieve, or what you already like, they have to guess, and guesses cost time and revision rounds.

A good brief does not need to be long. It needs to answer the right questions. Here are ten things worth including.

The 10-point checklist

1. About your business

A few lines on what you do, who you are and what makes you different. If you have brand guidelines, share them. If you already have a logo, colours or fonts, say so.

2. The purpose

What is this piece of design for, and what should it achieve? "A flyer" is a format, not a purpose. "A flyer that gets people to book a tasting this weekend" tells the designer what to prioritise.

3. Your audience

Who are you trying to reach? Age range, interests, where they live and what they care about all shape the design. Something that works for teenagers may not work for business owners.

4. Style and references

Share examples of designs you like and, just as useful, ones you dislike, with a sentence on why. Mention colours, fonts or moods that you want or want to avoid. References save a lot of explaining.

5. What you need delivered

Be specific about the formats and sizes: a poster for print, a square image for Instagram, a banner for your website. Say which file types you want, and whether you need editable files so you can make changes later.

6. Your budget

Agree a figure before work begins, so the designer can suggest what is realistic. Make sure the price includes at least one round of changes.

7. Timeline and milestones

Give a final deadline, and also any checkpoints along the way. Mention your own deadlines too, such as when you will supply photos, text and logos. If those arrive late, the schedule moves.

8. Who approves what

Say which stages need your sign-off. For example, you might want to see initial ideas before the designer polishes a full draft.

9. How you will communicate

Decide how feedback and files will be shared: email, messages or a shared folder. Agree it early, so nothing gets lost.

10. One person for feedback

Name a single person who gives feedback. If three people send three different sets of comments, the designer is stuck in the middle. Collect everyone's opinions first, then send one clear message.

A quick template

You can copy this into an email or message:

About us: (what you do, and who you serve) Project: (what it is and why you need it) Goal: (what it should achieve) Audience: (who it is for) Style: (links or examples you like and dislike) Deliverables: (sizes, formats, editable files?) Budget: (amount, and how many revision rounds) Timeline: (final date and any checkpoints) Content: (what you will provide, and by when) Contact for feedback: (name and how to reach them)

Giving feedback that helps

  • Be specific. "Make the heading bigger and the background lighter" is easier to act on than "I don't like it".
  • Say what is wrong, not how to fix it. "I can't read the date from far away" lets the designer find the best solution.
  • Respond at the agreed stages. Changes at the end, after approval, cost more than changes early on.
  • Keep the goal in mind. Ask whether each comment helps the design do its job, not only whether you like it personally.

The payoff

Ten minutes spent on a clear brief can save days of back and forth. It also makes it easier for a designer to give you an accurate quote, because the scope is clear from the start.
