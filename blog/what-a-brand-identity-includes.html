What a Brand Identity Actually Includes (and What You Should Receive)

A logo is only the start. Here is what a full brand identity includes, and the questions to ask so you know exactly what you are paying for.

CategoryBranding
Published17 Oct 2026
Reading time4 min

If you are hiring a designer for "branding", it helps to know what you are actually buying. Plenty of people ask for a logo, then find out later that they also needed colours, fonts and a way to use them all consistently. This guide explains what a brand identity usually includes, so you can compare quotes properly and ask for the right things.

A logo is the start, not the whole identity

A logo is one piece of a larger system. A brand identity is everything that makes your business look and sound like you wherever people meet it: your website, social media, packaging, invoices, signs and adverts. The goal is that someone can recognise you without seeing your name.

The core pieces

1. Logo variations

One logo is rarely enough. A good package includes a primary version, a simplified or secondary version for small spaces, and light (usually white) versions that work on dark backgrounds. It should also come with simple rules for clear space around the logo, minimum sizes, which backgrounds to use, and common mistakes to avoid, such as stretching it.

2. Colour palette

Your main and supporting colours, with exact values for screen and for print so they look the same everywhere. A thoughtful palette also takes readability into account, so that text stays easy to read on your brand colours.

3. Typography

The fonts you use and how to use them: what to use for headings, what to use for body text, and the sizes that go with them. Ask about licences. Some fonts are free for personal use only, and a business may need to buy a licence.

4. Supporting visuals

Icons, patterns, and the style of photos or illustrations that belong to your brand. These are what make your materials feel like they come from the same place.

5. Brand guidelines

A short guide (often a PDF or a web page) that pulls it all together, so that you, your team and anyone else you hire can use the identity correctly. For many small businesses, a few clear pages are enough.

6. Files and templates

The files you will actually use: your logo in vector and image formats, your colour codes, and details of your fonts. Many packages also include ready-made templates, such as social media posts, a presentation, or an email signature, so you can keep things consistent without a designer every time.

Extras that depend on the project

Some identity projects also include the thinking behind the visuals:

  • Strategy and positioning: who the brand is for and what makes it different
  • Messaging and tone of voice: how the brand speaks, with examples
  • Naming: coming up with, and checking, a business or product name
  • Brand architecture: how several products or services relate to one main brand

You do not need all of these. A new freelancer and a growing company with several services need very different things. What matters is that you agree what is included before work starts.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Exactly which files will I receive, and will I get editable source files or only exported images?
  • Are the fonts and any stock images licensed for my use?
  • How many rounds of revisions are included in the price?
  • Who owns the final work, and can the designer show it in their portfolio?
  • What is the timeline? Large agency projects are often quoted at 12 to 16 weeks, while smaller projects usually move faster, so ask for dates.
  • Will I get guidelines that explain how to use the identity?

A simple rule

If a quote just says "logo design", ask for the list of deliverables in writing. If it says "brand identity", ask the same thing. The clearer the list, the fewer surprises later, and the easier it is to compare one quote against another.
