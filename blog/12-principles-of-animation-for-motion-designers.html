The 12 Principles of Animation, Explained for Motion Designers

Disney's 12 principles of animation are over 40 years old and still make motion graphics feel alive. Here is each one, with a practical tip you can use today.

CategoryMotion
Published26 Oct 2026
Reading time4 min

If your animations feel stiff, robotic or "off" but you cannot say why, the answer is probably in a list written more than forty years ago. The 12 principles of animation were described by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas in their 1981 book The Illusion of Life. They were written for hand-drawn films, but they apply just as well to logo animations, explainer videos, UI motion and 3D work.

Here they are, with a way to use each one in motion design.

1. Squash and stretch

Objects with weight and flexibility change shape as they move, while keeping the same volume. A bouncing ball flattens a little when it lands and stretches as it speeds up. Try it: add a subtle squash on impact for a bouncing logo or shape. A little goes a long way.

2. Anticipation

A small movement before the main action prepares the viewer for it. A character crouches before jumping. Try it: have a button or title dip back slightly before it pops forward. The main move will feel more believable and more satisfying.

3. Staging

Present each moment so that the viewer looks at the right thing. Use position, lighting and camera angle, and leave out detail that does not matter. Try it: animate one important thing at a time. If five elements move at once, nothing gets noticed.

4. Straight ahead action and pose to pose

Two ways of working. Straight ahead means drawing frame by frame from start to finish, which gives fluid, spontaneous motion. Pose to pose means planning the key poses first and filling in between. Try it: most motion design is pose to pose, since you set keyframes and let the software fill in the gaps, but you still need to check the result. Straight ahead suits things like smoke, splashes and hand-drawn effects.

5. Follow through and overlapping action

When something stops, its loose parts keep moving for a moment, and different parts move at different speeds. Try it: offset the keyframes of secondary elements by a few frames, such as text lines, trailing shapes or a shadow, instead of starting and stopping everything together.

6. Slow in and slow out

Things speed up gradually and slow down gradually, rather than switching instantly to full speed. Try it: avoid linear keyframes. Use easing (Easy Ease in After Effects, for example) and shape the curve in the graph editor. This single habit makes the biggest difference to how professional motion looks.

7. Arcs

Natural movement follows curved paths, not straight lines. Try it: if an object moves between two points, give its path a gentle curve. Mechanical movement is the exception, which is why straight lines can make something feel robotic, if that is what you want.

8. Secondary action

Small supporting actions add life to the main one, without stealing attention. Try it: a glow that pulses as a logo lands, or a shadow that stretches as an object moves. If it pulls focus from the main action, tone it down.

9. Timing

The number of frames an action takes decides how fast it feels, and that tells the viewer about weight, mood and personality. Try it: fewer frames feel snappy and light, more frames feel heavy and calm. Play your animation at full speed, not only scrubbing, and adjust until it feels right.

10. Exaggeration

Push the motion a little beyond reality so it reads clearly and does not look dull. Try it: increase an overshoot or a scale change slightly beyond what feels natural, then pull it back until it feels lively but not silly. Restraint matters.

11. Solid drawing

Give forms volume and weight, so they feel three-dimensional. Try it: in 3D, this means understanding form and perspective. In 2D motion graphics, keep lighting, shadows and perspective consistent, so shapes feel like they occupy real space.

12. Appeal

The animated equivalent of charisma. Characters and shapes should be interesting and easy to read. Try it: check your silhouettes and keep designs clear and simple. If the viewer cannot tell what something is at a glance, simplify it.

A note on "moving hold"

Some lists add a thirteenth idea: moving hold. Even when a character is still, subtle movement such as breathing keeps it from looking lifeless. In motion graphics, a tiny drift or glow on a held frame does the same job.

Where to start

Do not try to apply all twelve at once. Start with easing (slow in and slow out), timing and anticipation. Those three improve almost any animation immediately. Then add overlap and secondary action as you get comfortable.
